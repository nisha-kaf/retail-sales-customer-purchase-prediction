Chart 1 — Monthly Revenue Trend

The monthly revenue shows a clear seasonal pattern from December 2009 to December 2011. Revenue generally increases toward October and November, suggesting stronger customer purchasing activity before the holiday season. After this peak, revenue falls noticeably around December and January. Overall, the chart indicates that the business experiences higher sales during the pre-holiday period and lower revenue immediately after the seasonal peak.


Chart 2 — Top 10 Products by Revenue

The chart shows the 10 products that generate the highest revenue. The revenue is concentrated among a relatively small number of products, meaning that some products contribute much more to total revenue than others. These high-revenue products are important for the business because maintaining their availability can help protect sales. They can also be considered for promotional activities because of their strong contribution to revenue.


Chart 3 — Top 10 Countries by Revenue

The chart shows the 10 countries contributing the highest revenue to the business. The United Kingdom generates substantially more revenue than the other countries, indicating that the business is strongly concentrated in the UK market. Other countries also contribute to revenue, but their contributions are comparatively smaller. This shows that the UK is the main market, while international markets provide additional revenue opportunities.


Chart 4 — Order Value Distribution

The histogram shows the distribution of order values, with the top 1% of extremely large orders excluded from the displayed range for better readability. Most orders have relatively low or moderate values, while a smaller number of orders have much higher values. This creates a strong right-skewed distribution with a long tail toward higher order values. These larger orders can contribute significantly to the overall revenue even though they occur less frequently.


Chart 5 — Customer Total-Spend Distribution

The histogram shows the distribution of total spending across customers. Most customers have relatively low to moderate total spending, while a smaller group of customers has much higher spending, creating a long right-hand tail. The top 1% of customers by total spend are excluded from the displayed range only to make the main distribution easier to see. This uneven spending pattern supports using a percentile-based definition for identifying high-value customers, rather than relying on one fixed spending amount.


Chart 6 — Quantity per Order-Line Distribution

The boxplot shows the distribution of the quantity purchased in each order line. Most order lines contain relatively small quantities, while some order lines have much higher quantities than the typical order. These unusually high quantities appear as outliers and may represent bulk purchasing behavior. The top 1% of quantities are excluded from the displayed range only to make the main distribution easier to interpret.


Chart 7 — Revenue vs Quantity

The scatter plot shows the relationship between the quantity purchased and revenue at the order-line level. In general, revenue tends to increase as the quantity increases, which is expected because purchasing more units usually generates more revenue. However, the relationship is not perfectly linear because products have different prices per unit. Some order lines with relatively low quantities can still generate high revenue when the products have higher unit prices. The chart is based on a sample of 5,000 order lines to make the visualization easier to read.


Chart 8 — Monthly Order Count

The chart shows the number of unique orders placed each month from December 2009 to December 2011. The monthly order count follows a similar seasonal pattern to the revenue trend, with order activity increasing around the peak sales period and decreasing afterward. This suggests that the higher revenue during the peak period is associated with a larger number of orders, rather than being caused only by a few unusually large orders. The chart therefore provides additional evidence of seasonal variation in customer purchasing activity.


Chart 9 — Revenue by Day of Week

The chart compares total revenue across the days of the week. Revenue is generally higher on weekdays, while Saturday shows a noticeable decrease compared with the other days. This indicates that customer purchasing activity varies depending on the day of the week. Understanding this weekly pattern can help the business plan staffing, promotions, and other activities around periods of higher or lower sales activity.


Chart 10 — Revenue by Hour of Day

The chart shows how revenue is distributed across different hours of the day using a 24-hour format. Revenue is concentrated during normal daytime business hours, with the highest activity occurring around the late morning to early afternoon period. Revenue is lower during the early morning and later hours. This pattern helps show when customer purchasing activity is strongest during the day.


Chart 11 — Top 10 Customers by Spendings

The chart shows the 10 customers with the highest total spending. The spending levels vary across these customers, but the chart highlights a small group of customers who contribute substantial individual spending. These customers represent an important high-value segment for the business. Their purchasing behavior can be monitored to support customer retention and loyalty activities.


Chart 12 — Share of High-Value vs Other Customers

The pie chart shows the proportion of customers classified as high-value compared with other customers. Customers at or above the 75th percentile of total spending are classified as high-value, so this group represents approximately 25% of the customer base, while the remaining 75% are classified as other customers. This creates two groups with different sizes, making the target moderately imbalanced for the classification task in the machine learning analysis.
